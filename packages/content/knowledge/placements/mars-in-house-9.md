The ninth holds Sagittarius in the natural zodiac — Jupiter's sign, and Jupiter is
Mars's friend — so the terrain is hospitable even though a malefic in a trine is
contested ground in the literature.

## Principle with force behind it

Dharma bhava is what the native holds to be true and the authority they accepted
voluntarily. Mars there is traditionally read as conviction that gets acted on: belief
defended, argued, travelled for. The classical associations are the martial side of
doctrine and of law — advocacy, reform, service, anything where a principle is enforced
rather than contemplated.

## Journeys with a purpose

The ninth's long-distance travel takes Mars's character: journeys undertaken toward
something rather than away from something, often with effort the native chose
deliberately.

## The father and the teacher

Both significations sit here, and the sources do not always separate them. Mars is read
as a relationship with accepted authority that has friction in it — disagreement
conducted in the open, and a native who tests a teaching rather than receiving it.
Material about the father deserves the same tentative framing as fourth-house material
about the mother: a reading is likely touching something undiscussed, and should leave
room to be wrong.

## Where the sources genuinely disagree

Classical opinion on malefics in trines is split, and this placement is where the split
is most visible. One position holds that a malefic in a dharma house damages the house's
grace; the other, that a trine is strong enough to employ a malefic rather than be
harmed by it. Neither settles the other, and a careful reading names which convention it
is using instead of presenting one as established.

## The caution

The specific risk is dogmatism — certainty held with enough force that it stops being
examined, which is the trait least able to notice its own change of state. Nothing here
supports a statement about anyone's religion, or about how a legal matter resolves.
